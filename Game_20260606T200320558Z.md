You keep moving. The eastern fork is smoother than the northern one — the sand packs harder here, the kind of road that gets used by people who know where they are going. The haulm's hooves leave cleaner prints in it. Behind you, Morrow's trail is fainter than it should be; the desert drinks what falls from him and gives nothing back.

Eleven tags. The weight in your coat shifts with each step — not evenly, not predictably. Sometimes a tag presses harder against the cloth as if the name written on it is straining toward something ahead. Sometimes all eleven go light at once, as if the road ahead is clearing in their memory.

Morrow is walking. He has not asked to stop. The road is taking from him in a way that has nothing to do with distance — you can feel the junction in the way he moves, the way he favors his left side where the sand-colored coat is going darker. He is paying in blood for every step east and the price is not evenly distributed. Some steps cost more than others. Some cost almost nothing. The road is not consistent. The road is deciding.

The haulm does not look back. Its stone head is set toward Lagota and the certainty in its stride is the same certainty it had leaving the stable — the compass-following, the road-knowing. It has walked this route before. It walked it twelve times before someone stopped coming back.

The horizon shifts. Not gradually — the way things shift when you stop expecting them to change. Lagota appears the way a wall appears when you have been walking toward it long enough: all at once, complete, suddenly the only thing the sky sits on. Walls. Guard towers. The shimmer of the conduit infrastructure at the city's edge — the harvesting apparatus, running hot, running always. The road leads straight to the gate and the gate has a checkpoint and the checkpoint has guards and the guards have ledgers.

Morrow stops.

"Not the gate," he says. His voice is thin in a way that has nothing to do with volume. "I never made it past the gate."

The haulm stops beside him. Its blue eyes are on the checkpoint, not on Morrow, not on you. The eyes of a creature that has been waiting at this exact point for longer than it can calculate.

"The junction took my routes," Morrow says. "But the junction is behind us. The checkpoint takes something else."

You look at the gate. The guards are watching the road. They are not watching you specifically — they are watching the road, the way guards watch the place where strangers arrive. One of them has a ledger open on a podium. The other is standing at the barrier with a long pole.

Eleven tags press against your chest. Eleven names that did not arrive. Eleven people who paid to be on this road and were stopped before they reached it. The tags are heavier now than they were at the junction. Proximity to Lagota adds weight.

"The checkpoint will want to know what you are carrying," Morrow says. He is looking at the gate, not at you. "It will want to log the tags. When it logs them, it takes the weight. That is the price of entry. Not the goods. Not the routes. The meaning. The weight of what you are carrying toward it."

He sways slightly. Catches himself on the haulm's cargo frame. The wood groans under his hand.

"I was carrying twelve routes," he says. "Twelve weights of meaning. The checkpoint logged them. And then I did not have them anymore. And then I did not have the strength to walk past the gate."

The tags press against your chest. Eleven names. Eleven people who did not arrive. Eleven weights of meaning that someone paid to carry toward Lagota and were stopped before they could deliver.

The haulm's eyes dim slightly. It is not looking at the checkpoint anymore. It is looking at Morrow.

"What do you do?" Morrow asks. His voice is very quiet. "The checkpoint will take the meaning if you go through. But the junction already took one name. So maybe the checkpoint only takes what is left."

The guard at the ledger looks up from his book. He is watching the road. He is watching you.

What do you do?

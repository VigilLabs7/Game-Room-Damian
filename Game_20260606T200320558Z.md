The blue has no bottom. No surface. No horizon. You hang in it and there is nothing to push against, no direction that is more true than another. You are aware that you exist but the shape of you hasn't decided what it is yet. The only thing that has form is the thing you are holding onto without knowing you are holding onto it — a single concept, a single truth, the thing you will become.

The blue moves around you. Not waves. Not currents. Just the endless same-blue, pressing in from every side, patient and total. You are not alone in this place. Every drifter is alone here — even when two are technically nearby they cannot find each other, cannot hear each other, cannot reach. The Ocean does not connect. It only holds.

There is nothing to do but wait. The question is whether you will wait as nothing, or whether the thing you are holding will take weight and become the thing that lifts you out.

You hear something. A voice, maybe. Or the memory of a voice. It is calling from above and it sounds like your idea sounds, like the thing you are clinging to. It says come up, darling.

The blue thins above you. Something is waiting on the other side.

What do you do?

## Turn 1

**Player:** I cling to the understanding that you cannot take what is not offered. The blue holds everything — but I am not here to be held. I move toward the thinning. I have the weight and I know what it costs.

**Game Master:**

The blue has a texture you did not notice before. Pressing back is not the same as not pressing at all. The thing you are carrying has edges now — it is not yet a shape, but it is the shape of a push, and you are using it. You move through the blue the way a hand moves through water toward something held just above the surface: clumsy, determined, aware that the reaching itself is a kind of proof.

The voice above is still there. Closer now. The thinning it promised is not a hole — the blue simply becomes less itself the higher you go, until what holds you becomes what you are, and that is the moment of becoming. You are not there yet. You are in the part where the blue notices you noticing it.

Someone is hanging above you in the same blue, arms spread, suspended in the waiting. They are not moving toward the surface. They are waiting for something to move through them first — the way a coin waits in a fountain, patient and tarnished and certain that someone will eventually reach. They turn their head slightly and you cannot tell if they see you.

The blue between you is thin enough to hear through. Not words. A feeling. The feeling of someone who has been here long enough to know what the surface sounds like when it breaks.

The thinning is above. The weight is yours. The cost is already paid — you paid it the moment you decided not to be held.

## Turn 2

**Player:** I stop noticing back. The blue can have its awareness — I have what I'm carrying and I'm not giving it up to be noticed. I push through the last layer. The voice is closer now and it sounds like the thing I decided was worth the cost. I reach.

**Game Master:**

You arrive.

Not the way arrivals usually happen — not tumbling, not gasping, not finding sand under your hands and a sun above. You arrive the way a word arrives when someone finally says it: all at once, completely, with the full weight of what came before it.

You are sitting on the rim of a stone fountain. The water in the basin is silver and continuous, impossibly clear after wherever you have just been. The sound of it reaches you before the rest of the world does — a soft, continuous chuckling that does not seem to come from the same world as the silent place you left. Your hands are flat on the stone. Your legs are whole. There is no injury, no sand in your lungs, no disorientation that comes from the violence of surfacing. The body you are in is the body you earned in a prior life. It remembers what it knows.

Three words are carved into the basin rim, facing outward toward the desert you did not come through: IAM NOTUM SUM. The letters are uneven, hand-cut, weathered — and they do not appear to age.

Someone is sitting on the rim across from you.

Not a stranger. Not quite a friend. A figure in clothes that have the weight of ledgers — ink-stained cuffs, the look of someone who has spent a life writing down what things are worth. They have a book open in their lap, or something like a book — the pages are dense with small, careful script. They are not the kind of figure the desert produces. They are the kind of figure the town produces, the kind that has always been here, the kind that knows the inscription by heart.

They look up from the page.

"Sable," they say. Not a question. The name sounds right in their voice, like they have been holding it for a while, waiting for someone to come and say it out loud. "You look like you know where you've been. The water's been high all morning."

The fountain hums. The desert waits behind you. The town waits ahead.

What do you do?

## Turn 3

**Game Master:**

The question lands the way a coin lands in a basin — with a sound that is quieter than the splash it makes. The figure does not flinch. They turn a page in the dense script, not as if they are reading it, but as if they are finishing a thought they had already finished.

"Who wrote me?" The figure's voice has the texture of old paper. "That is a question with a long answer and a short one. The short answer is that the fountain writes. The fountain holds a place for someone who knows the inscription, and the inscription has always been here, and so someone has always been here to sit with it."

They look up at you. Their eyes are the color of a seal stamp — dark, certain, carrying weight in a way that has nothing to do with hardness.

"The long answer is that the fountain was here before anyone sat at it. The words were carved before there was anyone to carve them. Someone came, once, and cut them into the stone with a blade and a great deal of certainty. I do not know who. The name of the carver is not in my ledger."

They close the book — or whatever the dense-scripted thing is — and set it on the rim beside them. The water behind them rises slightly, then settles.

"But you are not asking about me," the figure says. "You are asking about you. You came through the blue without tumbling. You arrived with a name and a cost already paid. The water knows what you are carrying — it has been high all morning, waiting." The figure gestures at the inscription facing the desert. "You know those words. You said them to get here. The fountain wrote you in the moment you said them. That is the answer to both questions at once."

The figure slides slightly on the rim, making room. Not an invitation to sit — not yet — but a rearrangement of space. The town waits beyond the square. The four landmarks wait: the general store, the stable, the church, the residential strip. Somewhere beyond those, the road to Lagota.

"You are Sable," the figure says, finally. "You arrived at the fountain. The question now is where you go from here."

What do you do?

## Turn 4

**Player:** I push off the rim and head for the stable. A town this size will have horses, and I need to know the road east.

**Game Master:**

You push off the rim and the fountain releases you without ceremony. The cobblestones are warm under your boots as you cross the square toward the stable's strip. The ledger-class figure does not call after you. The water keeps its sound to itself.

The stable is further than it looked from the fountain — a building hunched at the end of a commercial run, its door hanging open like a jaw that forgot to close. Hay dust hangs in the bar of light from the threshold. You step through and the smell changes: straw, animal warmth, the faint mineral edge of stone that is not quite stone.

Four stalls. Two are empty — gates hanging, straw scattered, nothing left but the shape of absence. The third stall gate is broken outward, splinters pale and fresh, the lock still bolted and intact on the hasp. Whatever left this stall did not unlatch it. It went through the wood.

The fourth stall is locked and occupied.

It is a horse the way a statue is a horse — the right shape, the wrong material. Stone hide, smooth and warm where it should be hide-warm. A tiny head perched on a long curving neck that makes up half its body. Blue eyes, dim like cooling embers. A cargo frame bolted to its back, worn into deep grooves from hundreds of loads it cannot carry anywhere. It watches you with the particular stillness of something that has stopped pretending to be alive but remembers the posture.

The haulem. You know the word without being told it.

There are no other animals. No stable hand. No one to ask about the road east except the stone horse that will not leave the cobblestone radius it is bolted to, and the empty stalls, and the broken gate of whatever got out.

From here the commercial strip continues north — the church with its crooked steeple visible beyond the stable's roof. The road east is not visible from the stable. You would need to go further, or ask, or find another way.

The haulem's eyes dim slightly. It does not move.

What do you do?

**Game Master:**

The grooves speak in a language of weight.

The cargo frame is iron-bolted to the back, the bolts set deep into the stone hide where stone hide meets cargo frame, and the grooves are cut into the frame's bearing surfaces the way a river cuts into a bank: by repetition, by time, by the patient persistence of weight in motion.

Seven loads that mattered more than the rest — or seven loads that were heavier, or seven trips where the road was harder than the others. The frame holds the memory of all of them, pressed into the iron the way a name is pressed into a page.

The haulem's eyes flare once as you study it. Not warning. Recognition. You are reading what it has carried and it knows.

The stone horse shifts its weight on the bolted legs. The cobblestone radius is visible beneath it — a circle worn smooth by years of the frame swinging at the end of a road that no longer comes. Someone drove this haulem to this radius, unbolted the cargo frame, and walked away. Not sold — the frame is still here. Not stolen — the frame is still bolted. Just stopped coming back.

The hay in the empty stalls whispers. The haulem's eyes hold you.

The road east is still not visible from here. But the question of the haulem is here, and it is yours now.

Its eyes flare. Not the dim ember-cool of before — a sharp, sudden blue, like a flame catching. The stone of its neck shifts as it turns to look at you, and the cargo frame on its back — still bolted, still grooved with seven hundred loads it carried before someone stopped coming back — swings slightly as the haulem takes one step forward.

The haulem stands in front of you with its head lowered, not the posture of a creature about to flee, and not the posture of a creature waiting to be told. Seven heavier loads. Seven loads that mattered. Someone brought this creature here, bolted it down, and walked away — not because they chose to leave, but because something stopped them. The haulem has been standing in that circle ever since, waiting.

The stable is quiet around you. The broken-gate stall to your left. The empty stalls to your right. The hay dust still hanging in the light. And the haulem, now free of the cobblestone radius, standing in front of you with its eyes open and its head lowered.
